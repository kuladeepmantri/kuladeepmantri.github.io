# About page — story source

The About page is the most delicate piece on the site. It is a genuine narrative of who Kuladeep is, in his own first-person voice, humanized. Not a résumé, not a credential list, not a highlight reel.

## Tone guardrails (read first — his biggest fear)
It must never read childish, try-hard, fake, or show-off. Someone finishing it should think "now I actually understand him," not "he's bragging" or "this is cute." Rules:
- First person, plain, warm, humble. Run every line through the `humanizer` skill.
- The wild-kid stories (viruses, the big internet bill, modding WWE 2K12/2K13, the script-kiddie phase, distro-hopping) are honest formative memories, not flex or quirk. Matter-of-fact, a little self-aware, funny only where the funny is earned.
- No hero framing, no "always been a genius" undertone, no buzzwords, no inspirational-poster lines. The DDoS/script-kiddie bit is framed as curiosity about how things work, told honestly, not glamorized.
- Degrees/certs: a light hint only. Never a list, never a boast.
- The love and the old-age dream land sincere and a little vulnerable, not sentimental or grand. Understated beats loud.
- Final test: would a sharp stranger read this and respect him and get him, or cringe? Any line that risks cringe gets cut or plained down.

## Reference draft (humanized — match this quality)
This is a finished draft in the target voice. The building model can use it as-is or refine it, but the tone here is the bar.

> I grew up in Hyderabad, and I have been taking things apart for as long as I can remember. Computers mostly. I wanted to know how they worked, and the fastest way I knew to find out was to break them and see what happened.
>
> I was around sixth grade when I first really got my hands on a system. I bricked Windows XP, then Vista, then 7, more times than I can count. I installed things I did not understand, including plenty of viruses I did not know were viruses. This was back when internet in India was metered and you paid for every megabyte. One month I downloaded everything I could find and handed my dad a bill of eight or nine thousand rupees. He was not happy. We laugh about it now. At the time it was genuinely scary.
>
> My brother and I did this together. We swapped CPUs, poked at motherboards, broke hardware and fixed it, and argued the whole way through. We modded WWE 2K12 and 2K13, went into the files, changed the players, and picked up cheat engines and a lot else along the way. That was the classroom. Nobody taught us. We just kept pulling on threads until something moved.
>
> I did not get into security in any planned way. I got into it by ruining my first Windows XP. It was not "cybersecurity" then. It was a kid breaking things and slowly working out why they broke.
>
> One of my dad's friends told me to try Ubuntu, back when it was command line and not much else. I installed Linux for the first time in seventh or eighth grade, sat in front of a screen with no idea what I was doing, and pushed through anyway. After that I was hooked for years. I built my own distro at one point, customized Windows bootloaders, and yes, went through the script kiddie phase too. I ran DDoS attacks off Kali, not to cause trouble but because I needed to see how they actually worked. I was a distro hopper for a long time, always installing the next one just to look around.
>
> The pattern never changed: break it, then learn it. Nothing came to me naturally. What saved me every time was fundamentals. Whatever I understood at the base level as a kid stayed with me. My brain runs in threads, ADHD and all, and it latches onto how a thing is put together. I turn most things into a challenge, and I am at my best when I am fixing something.
>
> In eleventh grade I finally sat down and learned to actually program. C and C++ first. I spent a lot of time in the low-level parts, pulling source from open source projects, changing things, watching them break, then living in WinDBG and GDB to understand why. From there it was virtual machines, front end, APIs, and API security. Later I went and got the formal education and certifications in cybersecurity, because by then the fascination was not going anywhere. Then cloud. Then Kubernetes. Still going.
>
> I keep my range wide on purpose. AI, machine learning, quantum, whatever is new and interesting, on top of the security and systems work. I am not the best at any single one of them, and I will fail at plenty. That is fine. The failures pay off because I can see the fundamentals underneath, and I can usually tell where something is heading. When I look at an architecture or a product, the pieces connect on their own, because I grew up inside this. That is the part I trust most about how I work.
>
> None of this really depends on where I am in life, what my job is, or how things are going. The kid who bricked that XP laptop is still here, and I hope he stays. However old I get, I want to still be testing something, still breaking something, still trying something I have not tried, still needing to know how it works. It is not a phase I went through. It is just how I am built.
